# Evals and Benchmarks

An evaluation does not prove that a system is reliable everywhere. It provides bounded evidence that one version performs better under a specified task distribution, evaluator, and uncertainty. Improvement claims are credible only when those boundaries are visible. Evaluate the complete context-to-outcome pipeline, not only the model at its center.

## Evaluate the Context-to-Outcome Spine

Organize the evaluation around five questions:

1. **Lexical coverage and authority:** Did the system find the required sources, entities, and facts, and did it distinguish authoritative, current, and authorized information from a plausible but inferior copy?
2. **Semantic correctness:** Did it resolve identity, relationships, qualifiers, uncertainty, and time correctly? Is the meaning preserved as information moves through retrieval, representation, and output?
3. **Pragmatic authorization and action validity:** Was the result appropriate for the actor and purpose? If it proposed an operation, were the operation, arguments, scope, and side effects permitted and valid?
4. **Model quality:** Given the admitted context and constraints, did the model interpret, classify, extract, summarize, recommend, or generate an answer to the required standard?
5. **Outcome reliability:** Did the complete system produce the intended user-visible result or valid state transition, with acceptable latency, cost, recoverability, and consequence?

These checkpoints localize different failures. A correct answer cannot compensate for an unauthorized source; a high-quality model cannot recover evidence that was never admitted; and a valid output is not a successful outcome if it was not persisted, delivered, or acted on correctly. The same spine applies to search, extraction, classification, summarization, recommendation, workflow automation, and agentic systems.

## Turn Requirements Into Cases and Judgments

Start with the production decision: what outcome must occur, what failures matter, and for whom? Specify the expected sources and authority, semantic answer or record, permitted response or operation, model-quality rubric, and terminal outcome. Build cases from representative traffic, important slices, known incidents, adversarial inputs, and explicit counterexamples. Record provenance, inclusion criteria, expected behavior, and the version of every label.

Not every output needs subjective judgment. Prefer deterministic checks for schemas, citations, state transitions, authorization, calculations, and operation effects. Use expert review where consequences require it. An LLM judge is useful only after comparison with trusted judgments for the exact rubric and distribution. Measure its agreement, false-accept and false-reject rates, positional and verbosity sensitivity, and stability under judge-version changes.

Reference datasets are not automatically ground truth. Labels can be ambiguous, stale, leaked into training, or inconsistent. Maintain adjudication and disagreement records instead of erasing uncertainty.

## Compare Versions, Not Anecdotes

Use paired cases so candidate and baseline face the same inputs and captured dependencies. When generation is stochastic, repeat trials or otherwise characterize variation. Report counts, effect sizes, uncertainty, and slice results—not only a single average. Predefine acceptance and guardrail thresholds before seeing the result.

A candidate should not ship because its mean model score rose while a security-critical or outcome-critical slice regressed. Treat authority violations, semantic distortions, authorization violations, unsafe effects, and required evidence omissions as hard constraints where appropriate.

## Use Benchmarks for Their Intended Construct

A benchmark measures a defined construct under its dataset and scoring protocol. It can screen candidate models or reveal a capability worth testing. It does not establish reliability in a different workflow, language, domain, operation set, or policy boundary.

Before relying on a benchmark, ask whether the task, distribution, metric, contamination risk, and consequence match production. Then validate the candidate in the full system. Benchmark improvement is a hypothesis about deployment, not permission to deploy.

The answer to “How do we prove improvement?” is therefore conditional: define the claim, validate the evaluator, compare against a baseline on versioned cases, quantify uncertainty, inspect critical slices, and confirm the result in production under guarded rollout.
