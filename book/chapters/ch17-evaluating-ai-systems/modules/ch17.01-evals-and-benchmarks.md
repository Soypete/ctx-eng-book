# Evals and Benchmarks

An evaluation does not prove that a system is reliable everywhere. It provides bounded evidence that one version performs better under a specified task distribution, evaluator, and uncertainty. Improvement claims are credible only when those boundaries are visible.

## Turn Requirements Into Cases and Judgments

Start with the production decision: what outcome must occur, what failures matter, and for whom? Build cases from representative traffic, important slices, known incidents, adversarial inputs, and explicit counterexamples. Record provenance, inclusion criteria, expected behavior, and the version of every label.

Not every output needs subjective judgment. Prefer deterministic checks for schemas, citations, state transitions, authorization, calculations, and tool effects. Use expert review where consequences require it. An LLM judge is useful only after comparison with trusted judgments for the exact rubric and distribution. Measure its agreement, false-accept and false-reject rates, positional and verbosity sensitivity, and stability under judge-version changes.

Reference datasets are not automatically ground truth. Labels can be ambiguous, stale, leaked into training, or inconsistent. Maintain adjudication and disagreement records instead of erasing uncertainty.

## Compare Versions, Not Anecdotes

Use paired cases so candidate and baseline face the same inputs and captured dependencies. When generation is stochastic, repeat trials or otherwise characterize variation. Report counts, effect sizes, uncertainty, and slice results—not only a single average. Predefine acceptance and guardrail thresholds before seeing the result.

A candidate should not ship because its mean score rose while a security-critical slice regressed. Treat authorization violations, unsafe effects, and required evidence omissions as hard constraints where appropriate.

## Use Benchmarks for Their Intended Construct

A benchmark measures a defined construct under its dataset and scoring protocol. It can screen candidate models or reveal a capability worth testing. It does not establish reliability in a different workflow, language, domain, tool set, or policy boundary.

Before relying on a benchmark, ask whether the task, distribution, metric, contamination risk, and consequence match production. Then validate the candidate in the full system. Benchmark improvement is a hypothesis about deployment, not permission to deploy.

The answer to “How do we prove improvement?” is therefore conditional: define the claim, validate the evaluator, compare against a baseline on versioned cases, quantify uncertainty, inspect critical slices, and confirm the result in production under guarded rollout.
