# Context Infrastructure Is the System

## The Label Hides the Work

“AI” describes too many different things: a model architecture, a product
surface, a workflow, or an entire company. That ambiguity is inconvenient for
engineers because systems with different models, data, retrieval, and
operational constraints get compared as though they were the same component.
When someone says “the AI failed,” the useful question is more specific: did
the system lack the right information, represent it incorrectly, or permit the
wrong use of an otherwise reasonable output?

Chapter 1 made context failure the first diagnostic, not a universal
explanation. This chapter maps the surrounding system so that we can locate a
failure before reaching for a model change. Reliable language systems are built
by engineering data, context assembly, state, authorization, execution, and
evaluation around a capable model. The same infrastructure supports agents,
but also search, extraction, classification, recommendation, summarization,
and workflow automation.

## A Model-Agnostic Discipline

Model capability matters, and models remain different in quality, cost,
latency, modality, and deployment requirements. Model rankings change with
the benchmark, task, release, and evaluation method. The durable claim does
not require calling models commodities: production behavior cannot be
delegated to the model alone. A system must still retrieve context, manage
state, represent meaning, enforce authority, and evaluate outcomes around
whichever model or non-generative NLP component it uses.

The useful analogy is to a data platform. Database engines remain meaningfully
different, but an application does not become reliable merely by choosing one.
Pipelines, transformations, governance, access patterns, and operations
determine whether the larger platform works. Models are not databases; the
analogy is that neither component can carry the reliability of the whole
system.

Consider a non-agent example: a support-ticket classifier must assign an
issue to a product and severity. The model may be excellent, yet the system
can fail if the product catalog is stale, “severity” has no shared definition,
or the classifier is allowed to emit a category that the routing workflow does
not recognize. A governed catalog and taxonomy supply the Lexicon, typed
labels and relationships supply Semantics, and the classification schema,
confidence policy, and routing rules supply Pragmatics. No agent loop is needed
for context engineering to determine the outcome.

## The Context Engineering Framework

Use three questions to inspect every layer of the system:

**Lexicon:** What sources, entities, definitions, and authorities are
available? Are they current, complete, provenance-bearing, and authorized for
this request?

**Semantics:** What do those entities and relationships mean in this domain,
task, and time? Are identity, schemas, taxonomies, and uncertainty explicit, or
must the model infer them from incidental text?

**Pragmatics:** What may this actor say or do with that meaning, and why? Are
the output schema, purpose, permissions, and downstream constraints enforced
outside the model?

Time cuts across all three: sources change, meanings are versioned, and
permissions or acceptable actions expire. Syntax is the representational
mechanism through which the lexicon and semantics become available to a model
or downstream NLP process.

The result is a context-to-outcome pipeline rather than a prompt trick:

```text
sources and authority → structured meaning → constrained outcome
       Lexicon               Semantics             Pragmatics
```

Prompt wording is one replaceable interface in that pipeline. Retrieval
quality, durable state, authorization boundaries, operational feedback, and
evaluation encode deeper knowledge about how the system works. Those
investments remain useful when the selected model changes.
