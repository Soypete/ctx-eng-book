# AI as a Marketing Category

## The Marketing Shell Game

Every six months, the technology industry invents a new category to describe what AI does. First it was "machine learning." Then "deep learning." Now just "AI"—as if the abbreviation alone explains everything. Each rebranding obscures more than it reveals.

The word “AI” now means everything from a model architecture on a phone to a warehouse-sized GPU cluster. It describes a mathematical technique, a product category, a marketing campaign, and a venture-capital thesis simultaneously. The ambiguity serves vendors whether or not it is deliberate. When everything is “AI,” comparisons collapse across systems with different models, data, tools, and operational constraints. The term becomes a shell game where the engineering work hides under a product label.

But for engineers building production systems, the ambiguity is a liability. When someone says "the AI failed," what actually failed? The model? The prompt? The retrieval system? The infrastructure? The authorization layer? These are fundamentally different failure modes requiring different solutions. Treating them all as "AI failures" guarantees we'll solve the wrong problems.

This chapter separates the signal from the marketing noise. Reliable AI systems are not built by tuning prompts alone. They are built by engineering infrastructure, data platforms, context assembly, and governance around a capable model. Production behavior emerges from the interaction among those layers.

**Building on Chapter 1:** We treated context failure as the first diagnostic, not a universal explanation. This chapter maps the system layers to inspect before concluding that model capability is the root cause.

## Why Models Are Becoming Commodities

In 2020, API access to GPT-3 represented a dramatic change in what software teams could build. Since then, smaller and openly available models have made useful language-model capability deployable in far more environments. That trend reduces dependence on any one provider, but it does not make models interchangeable or model quality irrelevant.

This follows a pattern we recognize from other infrastructure technologies. As a component becomes cheaper or easier to acquire for a class of workloads, differentiation shifts toward how the larger system uses it. Inference is not cheap or abundant for every model and deployment, but its falling cost makes the surrounding system more visible.

Model rankings change with the benchmark, task, release, and evaluation method. This book therefore makes no claim that models are commodities or roughly interchangeable; that comparison would require a dated evaluation of specified tasks, costs, and deployment constraints. The durable point does not require declaring a winner: a production system still has to retrieve context, manage state, authorize actions, and evaluate outcomes around whichever model it uses.

The data-platform analogy is useful if we do not take it literally. Mature database engines remain meaningfully different, but an application does not become reliable merely by choosing one. Pipelines, transformations, governance, access patterns, and operations determine whether the larger platform works. Models are not databases; the analogy is that neither component can carry the reliability of the whole system alone.

The implication is direct: prompt wording alone is a weak primary differentiator. Prompts can be versioned and tested, but they remain one replaceable interface. Retrieval quality, durable state, authorization boundaries, operational feedback, and evaluation encode deeper knowledge about how the product works. Those investments improve reliability even as the selected model changes.
