# Engineering the Context Boundaries

## A Systems Skill Set, Not a Job-Title Forecast

Context engineering names a body of work: building and maintaining the
information, state, authority, interfaces, and evaluations that let a language
system behave reliably. It applies whether the system uses a frontier model, a
small local model, a classical NLP component, or several of them. Teams may
organize this work across existing specialties or combine it in a small
product team. The durable unit is the boundary and its owner, not a proposed
title.

**Data and source engineering** builds pipelines that preserve provenance,
freshness, ownership, sensitivity, and source authority. Chunking and
embeddings are optional techniques, not the definition of the work. The output
is governed data; context is the task-specific view assembled from it.

**Retrieval and context assembly** fetches useful, current, and authorized
information for each request. Depending on the workload, that may include
lexical search, vectors, structured queries, graph traversal, reranking, and
result synthesis. The work balances task success, recall, precision,
freshness, latency, and cost.

**Semantic modeling** makes domain meaning explicit. Schemas, taxonomies,
entity resolution, relationship models, identifiers, and typed outputs reduce
the need for a model or downstream NLP process to infer organizational
concepts. This is the Semantics layer of the spine.

**State management** controls what persists across interactions and workflow
attempts. It includes session state, user preferences, durable execution
records, and workspaces, with schemas, lifecycle rules, consistency choices,
migrations, and recovery behavior. Orogat and Mansour frame long-term agent
memory as a data-management workload whose correctness concerns state
trajectories, and propose ingestion, revision, forgetting, and retrieval as
state-level operators [Orogat & Mansour, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26252).
That supports lifecycle-oriented state management; it does not establish a
distinct job title.

**Authorization and policy enforcement** builds the boundaries controlling what
a workflow or service can retrieve and execute. This includes role- and
attribute-based policy, delegated capabilities, data classification,
revocation, and audit. No system can promise that an AI never encounters a
bad input; the defensible goal is complete mediation, least privilege, denial
testing, and observable policy decisions. Chapter 11 grounds these mechanisms
in OAuth, OpenID Connect, and capability-security sources.

**Platform operations** runs the shared infrastructure: model or NLP serving,
deployment, cost controls, telemetry, incident response, and reliability
practices. OpenTelemetry can standardize traces, metrics, and logs; adopting
it does not by itself make a platform available or cost-effective.

**Evaluation** measures whether changes improve the intended behavior. It
includes dataset construction, scenario tests, component metrics, regression
detection, human review, and production feedback. Ask not only “is the answer
better?” but “which component changed, for whom, under what cost and failure
tradeoff?”

Small teams will combine these responsibilities, especially during discovery.
As scale, risk, and workload diversity grow, ownership should follow failure
boundaries: source quality, retrieval, semantics, state recovery,
authorization, platform budgets, and evaluation regressions. A boundary
without an owner becomes a boundary nobody tests.

The reliability consequence is organizational but concrete: a production team
should be able to name who owns the Lexicon, who maintains explicit Semantics,
and who enforces Pragmatics—even when one person temporarily owns several of
them.
