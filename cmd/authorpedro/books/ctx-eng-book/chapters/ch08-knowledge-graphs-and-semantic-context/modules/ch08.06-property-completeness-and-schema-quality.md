# Property Completeness and Schema Quality

## Ontology Precision vs Entity Precision

Precision in knowledge graphs means representing concepts at the right granularity for your data and domain. Start with what you have data for. If you only have the role of "employee" mapped, stop there.

Faculty Member → Research Professor makes sense when the domain needs that granularity. In university education, roles and responsibilities matter: office hours, classes taught, research tools. A professor's job is different from an adjunct's job. But in corporate finance, "employee" might be fine—it's just a cost center. The domain determines the depth.

The rule: model what your data supports. Don't design a beautiful ontology that your data can't populate. The ontology adds semantic relationships to your data. If you have data that you can relate within a domain, do it. If you don't, don't force it.

## Property Completeness Defined

**Property Completeness = Present properties / Expected properties per entity type**

Example: A Person entity has 4 expected properties (employer, education, nationality, advisor). If only employer exists → 25% property completeness. Low completeness degrades inference quality.

But "expected" is domain-dependent. In a university domain, a Professor might expect: office_hours, classes_taught, research_interests, department. In a corporate domain, an Employee might expect: department, title, start_date, manager. The expected properties come from the domain, not from some universal standard.

## Composing Subgraphs Into Complete Ontologies

You don't need one big ontology. You can have several small domains that combine into a complete picture:

- University of Utah might have an employment ontology (hiring dates, departments, titles)
- The School of Electrical Engineering has its own ontology (research groups, labs, courses)
- Professors exist in both—these are the linking nodes
- Pull in FOAF (Friend of a Friend) for the Person layer—a professor is a person, and FOAF gives you name, email, homepage

That's 3 graphs completing the whole picture of what a professor is and does. The semantics come from the composition.

This is how real knowledge graphs work: not one schema to rule them all, but subgraphs that intersect on shared entities. The employment graph knows about hiring. The academic graph knows about teaching. The FOAF graph knows about being a person. Together, you can answer: "What is Professor X's office hours and what research lab are they in?"

## Measuring Schema Quality

Track:
- Property fill rate by entity class
- Missing property analysis
- Optional vs required properties
- Property coherence: are related properties present together?

## The Completeness-Inference Relationship

More properties = more traversal paths = better reasoning. But only if those properties are actually populated. A schema with 20 properties at 10% fill rate is worse than a schema with 5 properties at 100% fill rate.

The practical approach: build incrementally. Start with the properties your data supports. Grow the ontology as you ingest more data. Measure fill rates. Let the data dictate depth, not the diagram.
